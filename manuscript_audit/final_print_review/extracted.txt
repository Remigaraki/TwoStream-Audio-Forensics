P21: Abstract
P22: Deepfake audio detectors are typically evaluated on uncompressed benchmark audio, while real distribution channels re-encode speech through lossy codecs that remove the fine spectral detail many detectors depend on. This study evaluates a two-stream neuro-symbolic architecture for deepfake audio detection under codec compression. A raw-waveform stream based on RawNet2 is combined with a statistical stream computing linear-frequency cepstral coefficients and PCA-compressed bispectral features, fused either by concatenation or by a cross-modal attention mechanism in which the statistical embedding conditions the neural embedding. Five primary configurations were trained on a stratified 125,123-utterance subsample of ASVspoof 5 and evaluated on an in-domain held-out test partition of 18,769 utterances under seven acoustic conditions spanning Opus, MP3, and AAC. All comparisons were made on identical utterance sets, permitting paired testing with McNemar’s test. 
P24: The proposed fusion model attains 0.81% EER on clean audio and remains below 2.4% under every codec condition. The controlled comparisons also identify which components account for this robustness. Codec-augmented training of the raw-waveform stream is the dominant effect, reducing error by 35% to 74% against an unaugmented control with p < .001 under all seven conditions. Fusion contributes a smaller improvement that holds on clean audio and all three Opus conditions but not under MP3 or AAC, and the proportional advantage is constant rather than widening under compression. The statistical stream degrades proportionally less than the unaugmented raw-waveform stream under most codec conditions, consistent with earlier reports that bispectral statistics tolerate moderate recompression, but it has the highest absolute error of any configuration throughout, so its limitation in this implementation is discriminative power rather than compression durability. minDCF reproduces the EER model ordering in six of seven conditions.
P26: Keywords: audio deepfake detection, spoofing countermeasures, codec robustness, lossy compression, data augmentation, multi-stream fusion, bispectral analysis, ASVspoof 5

P118: Chapter 1
P119: INTRODUCTION
P120: Advances in generative AI have made synthetic speech and voice conversion convincing enough that listeners can no longer reliably separate them from genuine human speech, enabling audio deepfakes that can reproduce a chosen speaker’s vocal identity, intonation, and delivery at high perceptual quality. This capability creates serious security and trust risks in authentication and trust-sensitive contexts such as voice-based verification, customer support, journalism, and public communication, where falsified audio can be used for fraud, defamation, and misinformation. While many deepfake-audio detectors achieve strong performance in controlled laboratory settings, real-world distribution channels rarely preserve “pristine” signals. Audio shared through messaging apps, social media platforms, Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) calls, and streaming pipelines is typically compressed using lossy codecs (e.g., Opus, MP3, AAC), which modify spectral content, suppress high-frequency detail, and introduce codec artifacts. These transformations can degrade the cues that detectors rely on, creating a gap between benchmark accuracy and operational reliability.
P121: This application study addresses the detection of artificially generated speech under realistic distribution conditions by proposing a two-stream neuro-symbolic detection architecture for both lossy and non-lossy environments. Rather than depending on a single representation, the model combines (1) a neural stream that learns discriminative artifacts directly from raw waveforms and (2) a signal-forensics stream that extracts interpretable statistical evidence relating to phase relationships and higher-order spectral structure. The two streams are fused to produce a single prediction. The study evaluates whether this combination, together with codec-augmented training, produces a detector that remains reliable after re-encoding, and identifies which of the two mechanisms contributes more to that reliability. The work seeks to make deepfake audio detection more dependable in uncontrolled deployment settings, where encoding conditions are unpredictable, and to clarify which forensic cues persist through compression and which do not. In this study, neuro-symbolic denotes pairing a learned neural representation with deterministic, closed-form signal statistics whose computation is fully specified and interpretable, rather than symbolic reasoning in the sense of logic-based AI.
P122: 1.1 Overview and Background
P123: In the current mainstream market, the use of artificially generated content has permeated content creation across all platforms, from text to audio and video, and from short-form to long-form content. Evidently, advances in generative speech and voice conversion have given rise to commercial voice-cloning and audio deepfake services that convincingly and methodically imitate a target speaker’s voice. These high-fidelity voice cloning and voice imitation services that are being offered by ElevenLabs, Hailuo AI (commonly known as MiniMax), and Cartesia produce near-indistinguishable voice manipulation that can even change the user’s native language to be used in content creation; these platforms further blur the line between what is authentic and the synthetic. As the case article by Barrington et al. (2025) states, most listeners cannot reliably tell a genuine voice apart from an AI-generated clone of that same speaker. Barrington et al. (2025) also examined naturalness, that is, whether listeners judge a voice to be real or AI-generated: participants correctly identified real voices 67.4% of the time, but correctly identified AI-generated voices only 60.8% of the time. This implies that the average person is vulnerable to the misuse of AI voice clones and synthetic voices.
P124: This shifts audio from a reliable indicator of identity into a vulnerable medium that can be manipulated for impersonation, fraud, and misinformation, enabling deceptive and harmful activities that endanger integrity and security. Moreover, the risk is amplified as deepfake audio is commonly distributed through platforms that automatically re-encode and compress, meaning the “audio that people actually hear” is rarely the same as the original source audio used in many scholarly evaluations.
P125: Prior research indicates that many detectable cues of synthetic speech are tied to specific frequency and structural patterns. According to Frank and Schönherr (2021), in their WaveFake study, neural vocoder outputs often exhibit detectable spectral differences concentrated in the higher frequency bands, and even simpler models can compete when those targeted bands carry the strongest spoofing evidence. This matters because real-world lossy codecs (e.g., Opus/AAC/MP3) commonly suppress or reshape high-frequency detail, which is exactly where some spoofing cues are strongest, causing a mismatch between “clean-benchmark detection” and “in-the-wild detection” scenarios.
P126: In addressing the limitations of relying solely on magnitude-based spectral representations, AlBadawy et al. (2019) argued that first- and second-order spectral features may be easy for synthesizers to match, whereas higher-order spectral correlations should be harder to adjust. They demonstrated that higher-order statistics, such as bispectral analysis, can reveal phase-coupling artifacts that conventional power-spectrum methods might miss. However, their work examined text-to-speech systems of that period and tested recompression only at 64 kbps and above, leaving open the question of how durable these higher-order cues remain under aggressive low-bitrate codecs. 
P127: In addition, contemporary approaches push detection directly from unedited audio, i.e., raw waveforms. Sun et al. (2023) showed that RawNet2-style models can learn neural vocoder “fingerprints” and even differentiate between vocoder architectures, suggesting that waveform-level learning can capture generator-specific traces beyond simple attack memorization. Regardless, waveform-learned traces can be obscured when compression and re-encoding smear fine-grained artifacts, meaning strong results on controlled data do not automatically translate to stable deployment performance.
P128: The broader literature reinforces that generalization is a central challenge in audio deepfake detection. The end-to-end deep learning framework, Audio Anti-Spoofing using Integrated Spectro-Temporal (AASIST) (Jung et al., 2021), highlights how spoofing cues can appear across spectral and temporal domains and proposes an integrated approach that avoids ensembles while achieving strong performance on anti-spoofing benchmarks. Moreover, Marek et al. (2024) show that deepfake detection performance can vary substantially across languages, underscoring that detectors degrade under distribution shifts even when the “task” remains the same. This generalization issue is directly analogous to codec-driven shifts: compression changes the input distribution and can invalidate assumptions learned from clean data. Finally, the FakeSound study (Xie et al., 2024) argues that the problem extends beyond speech into general audio manipulation and reports that human performance remains below 60% in accuracy, underscoring how difficult reliable detection is without robust forensic cues.
P129: In summary, existing studies establish that (1) artifacts exist and can be exploited for detection (Frank and Schönherr, 2021), (2) higher-order and phase-based evidence can reveal synthesis traces (AlBadawy et al., 2019), and (3) waveform-level models can learn vocoder fingerprints (Sun et al., 2023). A key gap remains: deepfake audio detection under realistic lossy distribution conditions is still insufficiently addressed, particularly in establishing which cues survive compression and whether combining complementary evidence sources improves reliability after re-encoding. AlBadawy et al. (2019) demonstrated phase-coupling artifacts in speech from text-to-speech systems of that period and found them robust to recompression at 64 kbps, but did not test aggressive low-bitrate codecs, leaving open the question of whether higher-order statistical evidence is durable in transit. This study treats that durability as an open empirical question rather than an assumption.
P130: 1.2 Statement of the Problem
P131: 1.2.1 General Problem
P132: Deploying current deepfake audio detectors has achieved significant benchmarks that, evidently, support their role in security and accuracy. However, these results came from laboratories with controlled environments, variables, and elements in their given context; thus, when deployed in real-world scenarios where these characteristics are far from their laboratory testing conditions, they often fail significantly. This critical failure mode is known as the Generalization Gap, and models such as RawNet2 and AASIST frequently fall victim to these persistent issues.
P133: Specifically, while these architectures excel at detecting artifacts in high-fidelity datasets (e.g., ASVspoof 2019), they struggle to maintain feature persistence when audio is subjected to “in-the-wild” conditions, such as transmission over Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) or via messaging apps such as WhatsApp. In these lossy environments, the high-frequency spectral cues that standard models rely on are often stripped away by codecs like Opus and MP3. Consequently, the core problem is not merely detection in a vacuum, but the lack of a robust, neuro-symbolic mechanism that can identify forensic signatures, such as quadratic phase coupling measured through the bispectrum, or neural vocoder fingerprints, that may persist through this compression process. Without bridging this gap, high laboratory accuracy remains a theoretical metric rather than an operational reality.
P134: 1.2.2 Specific Problems
P135: 1. To what extent does the performance of established audio deepfake detectors degrade under different lossy compression conditions (codec family and bitrate)?
P136: 2. Which detection cues are most vulnerable to lossy compression, specifically the high-frequency structural artifacts identified by Frank and Schönherr, and do linear-scale features (LFCC) offer greater stability?
P137: 3. To what extent do higher-order statistical indicators such as quadratic phase coupling, measured through the bispectrum, remain discriminative after compression, compared with learned waveform representations?
P138: 4. To what extent does the ability of raw-waveform detectors to identify neural vocoder fingerprints degrade as bitrate decreases across different codec families (Opus, MP3, AAC)?
P139: 5. Does integrating complementary evidence sources—waveform-learned representations and higher-order statistical cues—improve robustness compared with single-stream baselines when evaluated on clean versus compressed audio, and does any such improvement hold consistently across codec families?
P140: 6. How does codec-augmented training of the raw-waveform stream compare, as a source of compression robustness, with architectural fusion of the two streams?
P141: 1.3 Objectives of the Study
P142: This part presents the general and specific objectives to address the gaps and solve the problem(s) stated in the “Statement of the Problem.”
P143: 1.3.1 General Objective
P144: The general objective of this study is to design and evaluate a two-stream neuro-symbolic deepfake audio detection model that remains reliable in lossy environments by combining raw-waveform representation learning with statistical signal forensics.
P145: 1.3.2 Specific Objectives
P146: This study specifically aims:
P147: 1. To determine the extent to which audio deepfake detector performance degrades under different lossy compression conditions (codec family and bitrate).
P148: 2. To identify which detection cues are most vulnerable to lossy compression, specifically high-frequency structural artifacts, and to evaluate the stability of linear-scale features (LFCC).
P149: 3. To assess the extent to which higher-order statistical indicators remain discriminative after compression, compared with learned waveform representations.
P150: 4. To measure the degradation of raw-waveform detectors’ ability to identify neural vocoder fingerprints as bitrate decreases across codec families.
P151: 5. To evaluate whether integrating complementary evidence sources improves robustness compared with single-stream baselines across clean and compressed conditions, using EER and minDCF, with paired significance testing to establish whether observed differences are statistically distinguishable.
P152: 6. To isolate the contribution of codec-augmented training by comparing an augmented and an unaugmented raw-waveform model under identical conditions, and to compare that contribution against the contribution of architectural fusion.
P154: 1.4 Significance of the Study
P155: Current models fail, to varying degrees, to distinguish AI-generated voice recordings in lossy formats, because compression discards and alters fine detail in the signal. The significance pertains to the practical failure mode of deepfake audio detection in real deployment, where audio is routinely re-encoded and compressed by common platforms. By explicitly training and evaluating under lossy conditions, the work aims to produce a detection approach that better reflects how deepfake audio appears in real-world circulation rather than idealized clean-audio benchmarks.
P156: For security stakeholders and organizations that rely on voice-based trust, such as voice authentication workflows, customer support verification, and incident response teams, the study evaluates detection approaches under codec-induced degradation rather than on clean benchmarks alone. For digital forensic practitioners and platform moderators, it examines whether interpretable statistical evidence, specifically phase-coupling indicators and LFCC-based cues, retains forensic value after compression, and reports where it does not. For researchers and students in audio forensics, the study provides a structured seven-condition evaluation framework spanning three codec families, together with paired significance testing, and comparative results clarifying which cues remain stable under compression. A matched augmented and unaugmented control pair allows the effect of codec-augmented training to be separated from the effect of architecture, which is a distinction the existing literature on robust anti-spoofing does not consistently make. For the academic community, the work examines how far benchmark performance diverges from real-world reliability once codec-driven domain shift is introduced.
P157: 1.5 Scope and Delimitation/Limitation
P158: The scope of this study is limited to binary deepfake audio detection (bona fide versus spoofed) in lossy environments, using the publicly available ASVspoof 5 (2024) corpus. The work covers spoofed audio generated through the neural vocoder and voice conversion methods represented in that corpus, specifically attack types A01 through A16, and evaluates performance under a controlled robustness pipeline applying Opus (16, 32, and 64 kbps), MP3 (64 and 128 kbps), and AAC (128 kbps) compression. The proposed method is implemented as a two-stream architecture in which Stream 1 processes raw 4-second waveform segments, Stream 2 computes LFCC and bispectral features, and a fusion layer combines both streams for classification. Evaluation uses EER and minDCF, with McNemar’s test applied to paired predictions to assess statistical significance, and comparisons include single-stream and unaugmented baselines to quantify the contribution of each component.
P160: The delimitations of the study are as follows. It does not address deepfake detection outside the audio domain. It does not perform real-time or on-device detection, and no inference-latency measurements were collected; deployability is therefore discussed as a design consideration rather than an evaluated property. It does not cover every codec, platform processing chain, microphone condition, or adversarial manipulation strategy, and each codec family is represented by one to three bitrate settings rather than a full sweep. It does not localize manipulated regions within a file, treating each segment as a unit for classification.
P161: Two limitations bear directly on the interpretation of results. First, the partitions form an utterance-level random split, so attack types A01–A16 and speakers appear in both the training and test partitions of the subsample used here; the evaluation measures in-domain robustness to codec-induced distribution shift under known attacks and speakers, not generalization to unseen speakers or synthesis methods, and it is therefore not comparable to the unknown-attack protocol of the official ASVspoof 5 challenge. Second, each configuration was trained once, so no variance estimates across training runs are available, and small differences between configurations should be read with that in mind. Further limitations, including the incomplete training provenance of the fusion configurations, are discussed in Section 5.3.
P163: 1.6 Ethical Considerations
P164: This study acknowledges the dual-use implications inherent in audio forensics and spoofing countermeasure research. By systematically evaluating the degradation of deepfake audio detectors under lossy compression, and by identifying the codec conditions, notably low-bitrate Opus and MP3, under which the statistical stream’s discrimination degrades, this work maps architectural weaknesses that could theoretically inform adversarial evasion strategies. However, explicitly documenting these vulnerabilities under realistic Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) and streaming conditions is necessary to bridge the gap between idealized benchmark accuracy and operational reliability. The transparency of these findings is intended to assist security stakeholders in developing countermeasures that are robust to codec-induced distribution shifts, rather than providing a framework for generating undetectable synthetic speech.
P166: Furthermore, the scope of this evaluation is strictly delimited to the publicly available ASVspoof 5 (2024) corpus. Because this dataset is a benchmark purpose-built for detection research, the robustness pipeline relies exclusively on standardized neural vocoder and voice conversion methods, specifically attack types A01 through A16. This ensures that the study evaluates existing generative algorithms without non-consensually targeting, cloning, or manipulating the voice of any real individual.

P170: REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE
P171: 2.1 Signatures of Neural Vocoders and Synthetic Speech Artifacts
P172: Xie et al. (2024) expanded the definition of the deepfake threat landscape beyond human speech to include “general audio,” such as environmental sounds and background noises. They introduced the FakeSound dataset and a new evaluation protocol for identifying manipulated regions within complex audio scenes. A key finding of their study was that human listeners struggle with this task, achieving an average accuracy of below 60% (i.e., near random guessing). This result empirically demonstrates that modern generative audio has passed the “uncanny valley” of human perception, rendering human verification no longer a viable security strategy. They argued that detection systems must therefore evolve to focus on forensic traces that are invisible to the human ear but detectable by algorithms.
P173: This reference provides the strongest argument for the study’s significance. It contextualizes the thesis within the broader “arms race” of audio forensics, moving beyond simple voice cloning to a future of fully synthetic audio environments. The paper’s finding that human detection is unreliable directly motivates the need for the “Microscope” Stream (Stream 2), a module dedicated to detecting mathematical anomalies (such as quadratic phase coupling measured through the bispectrum) that no human listener could perceive. Whether such anomalies remain detectable after the audio has been re-encoded is the question this study puts to test. Furthermore, their call for “generalizable” detectors that work across diverse audio sources supports the thesis’s focus on robustness, validating the shift away from clean-lab benchmarks toward testing on “in-the-wild,” degraded, and compressed audio signals.
P174: When an AI imitates and mimics human voices, it often leaves traces or imprints in its output, like an artist leaving a signature or a video editor leaving a watermark. Synthetic generation likewise leaves jitter and acoustic inconsistencies that can serve as markers for identification. Furthermore, for long synthetic recordings, automated detection is necessary to keep analysis efficient, and such automation should rely not only on predetermined rules but on what is learned from previously seen data (Di Pierno et al., 2025).
P175: The authors of this paper carried out a detailed analysis of audio produced by six contemporary neural generative models (including MelGAN, Parallel WaveGAN, and WaveGlow) (Frank and Schönherr, 2021). In a rigorous frequency analysis, the authors found that neural vocoders tend to exhibit detectable spectral differences and structural artifacts concentrated in the higher frequency bands, with high-frequency information proving indispensable to detection. They also found that a simple linear model (a Gaussian Mixture Model) could match or exceed a complex deep learning model (RawNet2) in robustness: the GMM remained near-perfect under a simulated phone-recording channel, while RawNet2’s error rate rose sharply under that same degradation, despite RawNet2 performing better on average under clean conditions.
P176: The paper serves as a baseline for one reason in particular: it establishes that high frequencies are a weakness of neural vocoder output. (The WaveFake corpus introduced in that paper is not used in this study, which draws exclusively on ASVspoof 5; the paper is cited here for its frequency-domain finding.) This is the direct motivation for using LFCCs (Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients) instead of MFCCs (Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients) in Stream 2, and the reason why the Robustness Pipeline is necessary: lossy compression algorithms such as Opus explicitly target these high frequencies, so the durability of LFCC-based evidence under such compression must be tested rather than assumed.
P177: AlBadawy et al. (2019) examined the limitations of standard power-spectrum analysis for detecting synthesized speech. Using higher-order statistics (HOS), specifically bispectral analysis, they observed higher-order spectral correlations in synthesized speech that are not necessarily audible and that the power spectrum cannot reveal, and they attributed these correlations to the synthesis networks themselves, hypothesizing that long-range temporal connections in those architectures produce them. This paper supplies the empirical basis for the “Microscope” stream (Stream 2): it establishes that phase coupling is a detectable synthesis artifact under the conditions AlBadawy et al. tested. They reported that the bicoherence statistics remained discriminative after recompression at 64 kbps, and at 128 kbps when only low levels of noise were added, but they examined text-to-speech systems of that period (Amazon Polly, Apple, Baidu DeepVoice, Google WaveNet, and Lyrebird, plus six GAN-generated samples) and did not test low-bitrate conversational codecs such as Opus at 16 kbps. This study therefore treats the durability of phase-coupling evidence under aggressive compression as an open empirical question rather than an established property, extends the approach to the attack systems represented in ASVspoof 5, and tests it under aggressive codec compression directly. Chapter 4 reports the result of that test.
P179: 2.2 Representation Learning for Audio Forensics
P180: Identifying the hidden, inherent elements of an audio waveform is often a weakness of standard AI detectors: they lack methods that examine the underlying features and the factors that accompany them, which together carry evidence that no human listener can detect in a single hearing. The challenge of scanning through hours of audio can therefore overwhelm a system and produce an error rate detrimental to an organization’s security, creating a vulnerability that malicious groups can exploit. On its own, RawNet2 did not outperform the LFCC-based baseline on pooled results, but it detected the most difficult attack (A17) far better, and fusing it with the LFCC-based countermeasures gave the second-best result reported on the ASVspoof 2019 logical access condition (Tak et al., 2021). This complementarity between learned waveform features and hand-crafted cepstral features is the premise of the two-stream design. 
P181: Jung et al. (2020) proposed a significant architectural refinement to the RawNet family of models, designed to directly extract speaker embeddings from raw waveforms without pre-computed spectrograms. They introduced two key innovations: feature map scaling, which applies a sigmoid-based attention mechanism to scale the importance of different filter outputs, and the use of Sinc-convolution layers (Ravanelli and Bengio, 2018) in the first block. Unlike standard convolutions that learn arbitrary filters, Sinc-convolutions are initialized as band-pass filters, forcing the network to learn physically interpretable frequency sub-bands from the very first layer. They demonstrated that these “raw-waveform front-ends” could capture fine-grained micro-temporal cues that are typically smoothed out or lost during standard Mel-spectrogram conversion.
P182: This paper acts as the technical blueprint for Stream 1 (“The Ear”) of the proposed thesis. It indicates that raw waveform encoding is a viable alternative to spectrogram inputs for capturing fine-grained micro-temporal structure. The Sinc-convolution layer described in this work is particularly relevant to the Problem Statement regarding “high-frequency artifacts”, as it allows the neural network to explicitly focus on specific frequency bands where neural vocoder fingerprints are known to hide. This justifies the decision to use a RawNet-based encoder as the “neural” half of the two-stream architecture, ensuring the model has access to the rawest possible signal evidence.
P183: Going beyond identifying deepfake audio in general, Sun et al. (2023) postulated that the characteristic of fake audio that is easiest to detect is the fingerprint of the Neural Vocoder (the mechanism that converts spectrograms to audio). They applied a Multi-Task Learning framework based on RawNet2, a 1D-CNN learnable to directly consume raw waveforms. They found that the RawNet2 was capable of learning to detect the architecture of a particular vocoder (e.g. whether a clip was generated by High-Fidelity Generative Adversarial Network (HiFi-GAN) or WaveGlow), not just by memorizing particular spoofing attacks.
P184: This study acts as an architectural design for Stream 1 (“The Ear”). It affirms that RawNet2 is a strong baseline for waveform-level artifact detection compared with image-based CNNs (such as ResNet). This makes it a suitable baseline, as its direct waveform processing avoids the information loss inherent in standard spectrogram conversion, thereby preserving the fine-grained micro-temporal cues critical for detecting deepfakes. This thesis builds on that work, combining their RawNet2 methodology with the bispectral analysis of AlBadawy et al. (2019) to test whether a statistical stream can address RawNet2’s weakness when vocoder artifacts are smeared by compression.
P185: Jung et al. (2021) addressed the limitation of prior architectures that treated temporal and spectral cues as separate, isolated features. They hypothesized that critical spoofing artifacts often manifest in the relationships between specific time frames and frequency bands, which standard Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) fail to capture efficiently. To solve this, they proposed a novel heterogeneous graph attention network that models the raw audio signal as a graph, with nodes representing different spectral and temporal intervals. By using a “stack node” to aggregate information and a “max graph operation” to filter non-informative edges, they created a unified model that jointly learns discriminative patterns across both domains. Their results demonstrated a 20% relative reduction in error rates against the strongest baselines then available, confirming that deepfake detection improves significantly when the model is forced to look at spectro-temporal interactions rather than static features.
P186: This paper serves as the primary baseline for modern “integrated” detection architectures. It is highly relevant to this thesis because it supports the core premise of the two-stream architecture: that a single view of the data (e.g., just a spectrogram) is insufficient for robust detection. While AASIST integrates time and frequency via graph attention, this study proposes a neuro-symbolic integration (RawNet + Statistics). The success of AASIST’s heterogeneous approach supports the thesis design choice to fuse complementary streams—specifically, combining the micro-temporal learning of Stream 1 with the statistical rigor of Stream 2—to achieve a similar holistic view of the audio signal.
P188: Recognizing the usefulness of the combination of various visual representations of audio to enhance detection accuracy, the researchers did not use one type of feature, but instead extracted them using a variety of spectrogram variants, namely Constant-q Transform (CQT) and Mel-spectrograms, and fed them all into an ensemble of heavyweight Convolutional Neural Networks (including ResNet and MobileNet). Their results showed that each model and feature set could not cover all types of spoofing attacks (Pham et al., 2024). They achieved substantially higher detection rates on the ASVspoof dataset by averaging predictions from multiple deep models (Ensemble Learning) than with single baselines, demonstrating that different feature perspectives are required for reliable detection.
P189: The paper is a critical background to the Fusion Layer offered in this thesis. Pham et al. show that no single view of the data covered all attack types of the data. Nonetheless, Pham et al. did this with a computationally expensive collection of large image classifiers (ResNet/MobileNet), whereas the proposed thesis suggests a more efficient alternative called Neuro-Symbolic. Rather than combining two or more heavyweight deep learning models, this study pairs a single raw-waveform model (RawNet2) with a deterministic statistical feature extractor (bispectrum with PCA reduction), on the hypothesis that multi-view diversity can be obtained at lower computational cost. No inference-latency measurements were collected in this study, so the cost advantage remains a design rationale rather than a validated claim; deployability is discussed as such in Chapters 3 and 5. 
P190: Wang et al. (2025) presented the official summary and protocols for the ASVspoof 5 Challenge, the fifth iteration of the global standard benchmark for audio deepfake detection. Unlike previous versions (2019/2021), which focused largely on older Text-to-Speech (TTS) and Voice Conversion (VC) algorithms, ASVspoof 5 introduced a considerably harder dataset built from current-generation neural vocoders, diffusion models, and end-to-end speech synthesis systems. Critical to this iteration was the focus on “adversarial robustness” and the inclusion of data specifically designed to fool standard countermeasures, ensuring that high detection scores on previous datasets would not falsely predict success on modern attacks.
P191: This reference is foundational to the thesis as it serves as the primary source of the experimental data. It also establishes the difficulty of the task: because ASVspoof 5 contains high-fidelity fakes that are nearly indistinguishable to the human ear, it motivates the “Neuro-Symbolic” architecture proposed in this study. Furthermore, the protocols defined in this paper supply the reference definitions for the evaluation metrics (EER and minDCF) used in Chapter 3, including the Track 1 detection-cost parameters. The evaluation reported here is not, however, run under the official challenge protocol: as stated in Sections 1.5 and 5.3, this study uses a stratified subsample in which attack types A01–A16 and speakers appear in both the training and test partitions, making it a known-attack, in-domain evaluation. Results are therefore not directly comparable to published ASVspoof 5 challenge figures.
P192: 2.3 Robustness and Generalization Under Domain Shift
P193: Detecting AI-generated voice audios has been solved relatively well with varying scores across different models. What current models fail to perform at their fullest capacity is the problem of audio within lossy environments, which affects the inherent details and attributes within the waveform that essentially affect the overall accuracy and optimization of the model to further detection. These changes in the raw audio make signatures of synthetic origin more difficult to identify. Moreover, these kinds of gaps are further extrapolated and cross-referenced by adding layers that act as an additional layer or barrier and the integration of other methods such as pattern recognition, watermark identification, etc.
P194: To address these vulnerabilities, the use of FFmpeg to generate lossy-compressed audio (Opus, MP3, AAC) represents a validated data augmentation strategy, with recent literature providing strong support for this specific pipeline. Lu and Ebrahimi (2024) demonstrate, in the context of image and video deepfake detection, that assessing detectors under realistic processing distortions—including compression—is necessary because standard benchmarks understate real-world degradation; the present study applies the same principle to the audio domain using FFmpeg-based codec transcoding. Additionally, Zhang et al. (2024) show that training with a diversified set of audio corruptions, combined with frequency-selective adversarial training, improves detector robustness, and report that their detector was minimally affected by MP3 and AAC compression. By employing this codec-augmentation methodology, the present study is consistent with the established practices of the ASVspoof consortium (Liu et al., 2021), so that the simulated domain shift approximates conditions in modern telecommunications.
P197: Marek et al. (2024) investigated the “generalization gap” of deepfake detectors, specifically examining whether models trained on English-centric datasets can successfully detect deepfakes in other languages (e.g., German, Spanish, Slavic languages). Their hypothesis was that detectors often overfit to the linguistic characteristics of the training data rather than learning forensic artifacts in a language-agnostic manner. Through extensive cross-lingual benchmarking, they found that detection performance degrades significantly when the target language changes—a phenomenon they classified as a domain shift. They demonstrated that while some architectures, such as Wav2Vec 2.0, are more robust, most standard detectors fail when the input distribution (language) deviates from the training distribution. This study is critical for the “Robustness Pipeline” of this thesis. It frames domain shift as the central failure mode for real-world deployment. In the context of this research, lossy compression is treated as a direct analogue of the “language shift” observed by Marek et al.; both represent a fundamental change in the statistical distribution of the input signal. By demonstrating that detectors are sensitive to changes in language, this paper provides theoretical justification for testing detectors against changes in codec and bitrate.
P198: Addressing this generalization issue, Zhang et al. (2024) built DeepFakeVox-HQ, a 1.3-million-sample dataset drawn from 14 deepfake sources, and showed that existing detectors lose accuracy on diverse data and lose more under realistic corruptions and adversarial attacks. They found that a diversified set of augmentations improves robustness and that the strongest detectors rely on high-frequency features an attacker can manipulate, which motivated their Frequency-Selective Adversarial Training (F-SAT). The main theoretical rationale of the “Robustness Pipeline” is this paper. It supports the decision to train not only on clean ASVspoof data but also on codec-compressed variants, extending its finding that diversified augmentation improves robustness to the codec conditions this study targets. 
P199: However, training alone is not enough if the architecture itself is flawed. The issue with current models is that they are not clearly defined and can be classified as black boxes; as such, it is hard to determine, without human supervision and reinforcement, whether the model’s findings are accurate. Evidently, this was also the position of Haq et al. (2024), further outlining that binary classification is indeed not adequate for these kinds of sophistication regarding forensic tasks that involve voice cloning and manipulation. Inherent limitations were found to struggle in practical applications where compression and added stressors can further mute the elements present in the data. Furthermore, in a related study that involves forensic imaging, Siopi et al. (2023) noted that a singular stream or mechanism is subpar in today’s landscape due to the fact that the majority of the data’s natural visual traces get distorted. This vulnerability to distortion necessitates a departure from uni-modal reliance; when the primary signal is degraded, the detector effectively operates blindly. Consequently, to mitigate this failure mode, the literature advocates for a composite architecture that does not hinge on a single, fragile feature set.
P200: Consequently, to bridge this divide, several lines of work point toward combining complementary evidence sources, as in the LFCC and RawNet2 fusion of Tak et al. (2021) and the multi-feature ensembles of Pham et al. (2024), which motivates the two-stream neuro-symbolic architecture examined here. Where standard “black-box” architectures falter against the domain shifts of compression and unseen codecs, as the generalization failures reported by Marek et al. (2024) illustrate, a two-stream architecture that fuses the micro-temporal precision of raw-waveform analysis with the mathematical structure captured by bispectral statistics is a candidate countermeasure. Whether that architecture in fact delivers the expected robustness, and whether the bispectral component is the durable half of it, are the questions this study was designed to answer. This synthesis is also intended to address the explainability deficit inherent in binary classification while targeting the high-frequency artifacts identified as a signature of modern neural synthesis. P205: 2.4 Conceptual Architecture
P207: Figure 2.1 Overall conceptual architecture.
P208: Figure 2.1 presents the conceptual framework of the proposed two-stream neuro-symbolic architecture, that is designed to tackle the generalization gap common in the existing deepfake audio detectors. This framework, in contrast to traditional ones that only use deep representation learning, pairs learned and deterministic statistical representations, imposing a guided and mathematical view of feature persistence on it. The architecture is logically divided into three sequential facilities in order to provide integrity of the outputs, the Input Phase to control degradation, the Process Phase to extract the features of both streams and fuse them, and lastly, the Output Phase to evaluate performance and statistical significance rigorously. 
P210: The execution of this in a structured way down the line ensures that the framework transcends the normal superficial augmentation of data. The systematic redirecting of the stream of raw audio through an early degradation landscape causes a domain shift to occur intentionally before even the data is transmitted to the core network. This is designed to test whether the parallel tracks, balancing learned neural embeddings against deterministic statistical forensics, mitigate the generalization gap, and if so, which of the two mechanisms is responsible. Ultimately, this general architecture aims to map the compounded network models into a unified mathematical state, exploring whether the system can sustain robust detection performance under fluctuating, lossy telecommunication conditions.
P213: Figure 2.2 Input phase of the conceptual architecture.
P214: Figure 2.2 shows the Input Phase, which implements the domain shift and data ingestion: raw audio from conventional datasets and benchmarks is routed directly to the customized robustness pipeline. This is a controlled condition that produces the intended domain shift, and it draws on the ASVspoof 5 (2024) corpus, which provides both bona fide and spoofed audio across attack types A01–A16. This degradation of these standard benchmarks is, in effect, tantamount to subjecting the raw waveforms to the extensive degradation they would otherwise experience in the actual telecommunication networks, wringing the data out of them before it even arrives on the processing streams.
P216: After being diverted into this pipe, the audio is not just diverted to basic additive noise augmentation. Instead, aggressive lossy transcoding is applied by the framework using FFmpeg and pydub wrappers to simulate true transmission loss. The samples were tested with particular highly compressed bitrates, Opus of 16 to 64 kbps, MP3 of 64 to 128 kbps, and AAC of 128 kbps. This process of intentionally destroying high-frequency spectral artifacts is an important step in the methodology; it removes the superficial acoustic textures that the standard deep learning models memorize and rely on excessively, effectively removing the surface-level acoustic cues the model would otherwise default to, so the study can test whether the architecture instead learns to depend on more structural, potentially compression-invariant features downstream.
P219: Figure 2.3 Process phase of the conceptual architecture.
P220: Within the Process Phase (Figure 2.3), the architecture establishes a unified feature representation through a two-step fusion procedure. First, the statistical embedding from Stream 2 Estat is used as a query into the neural embedding from Stream 1 Eraw through a Cross-Modal Attention Mechanism, which acts as a dynamic feature selector (Vaswani et al., 2017). By weighting the components of the neural representation according to where statistical anomalies are strongest, the attention mechanism grants the model the capacity to shift its trust between the two streams depending on the acoustic artifacts present.
P222: Second, the attention-weighted neural embedding is concatenated with the statistical embedding, a standard strategy for combining multi-modal data streams (Baltrušaitis et al., 2018; Lahat et al., 2015), so that the statistical evidence is preserved directly in the fused vector rather than only as a weighting signal. The order of these two operations is load-bearing and is specified formally in Section 3.5. The fused representation is then passed to the Sigmoid Classifier for the final prediction. A second configuration replaces the attention step with plain concatenation of the two embeddings, isolating the contribution of the attention mechanism itself.
P225: Figure 2.4 Output phase of the conceptual architecture.
P227: At the last component of the two-stream pipeline (Figure 2.4) are the validators and the metric translators of the model’s capabilities and its facilities in handling and detecting what is required. Specifically, the fused network representations culminate in a definitive binary classification, calculating a final decision boundary on continuous scores to determine whether the ingested audio sample is a real (bona fide) utterance or a fake (spoofed) generation. To rigorously quantify the architecture’s empirical robustness against the induced Domain Shift from the robustness pipeline, this phase employs standard forensic evaluation metrics. The primary evaluators utilized are the Equal Error Rate (EER) and the minimum Detection Cost Function (minDCF), which serve as the objective benchmarks for evaluating whether the cross-attention mechanism prioritizes feature persistence and narrows the generalization gap. The tandem detection cost function (t-DCF) was specified in the original research design but requires an automatic speaker verification subsystem that this standalone countermeasure does not include; minDCF, the designated metric for ASVspoof 5 Track 1, is substituted, as explained in Section 3.7.
P229: Because the incorporation of higher-order statistics, specifically bispectral analysis, carries an inherent O(N²) computational load, the architecture applies PCA dimensionality reduction to keep the statistical stream tractable. This is a design rationale rather than a validated deployment property: no inference-latency or real-time-factor measurements were collected in this study, and computational feasibility is identified as future work in Section 5.4 rather than treated as an evaluated criterion here.

P232: METHODOLOGY
P233: 3.1 Research Design Overview
P234: This study adopts a Two-Stream Neuro-Symbolic Architecture designed to detect neural vocoder artifacts in audio, specifically targeting scenarios involving voice conversion and lossy compression. Unlike end-to-end approaches that treat audio as a black box, the proposed method integrates representation learning (Stream 1) with signal forensics (Stream 2).
P235: The methodology is divided into three core phases:
P236: 1. Robustness Pipeline & Data Augmentation: Simulating “in-the-wild” compression artifacts (Opus, MP3, AAC).
P237: 2. Feature Extraction & Two-Stream Modeling: Parallel processing of raw waveforms and high-order statistical features.
P238: 3. Fusion & Classification: A neuro-symbolic fusion layer to synthesize the final prediction.
P240: To accommodate the computational overhead of the bispectral analysis and raw waveform ingestion, all model training, feature extraction, and evaluation workflows across these phases were accelerated and executed utilizing NVIDIA T4 GPUs.
P241: 3.2 Dataset and Robustness Pipeline
P242: 3.2.1 Data Selection
P243: All experiments use the ASVspoof 5 (2024) corpus. The data comprises:
P244: · Bona Fide Audio: Genuine human speech recordings.
P245: · Spoofed Audio: Audio generated by neural vocoders and voice conversion systems, covering attack types A01 through A16.
P246: A stratified subsample of 125,123 utterances was drawn from the combined training and development partitions using a fixed random seed (42). All available bona fide utterances were retained, and spoofed utterances were drawn evenly across the sixteen attack types so that attack diversity is preserved rather than dominated by whichever attacks are most numerous in the source corpus.
P247: The subsample was partitioned 70/15/15 into training (87,585), validation (18,769), and test (18,769) sets, stratified on the class label so that the bona fide proportion of approximately 40% is held constant across all three partitions. The validation partition is used for checkpoint selection and hyperparameter decisions; the test partition is held out at the utterance level and used only for the final evaluation reported in Chapter 4. It contains 18,769 utterances (7,520 bona fide and 11,249 spoofed).
P248: Partition assignment is deterministic and verifiable. A SHA-256 digest computed over the sorted utterance identifiers paired with their split assignments yields the reference value ed4808bb0456de26. This digest was recomputed and checked at the beginning of every evaluation session; a mismatch would indicate that the manifest no longer corresponded to the data on which the models were trained, and would invalidate that session’s results.
P249: Scope of the evaluation protocol. Attack types A01–A16 appear in both the training and test partitions of this subsample. The test set is therefore a known-attack evaluation, not the unknown-attack protocol used in the official ASVspoof 5 challenge, where evaluation attacks are withheld from training. Because the partitions form an utterance-level random split, speakers are not held out either, and the test partition is not the official ASVspoof 5 evaluation set. Results in this study are therefore in-domain: they measure robustness to codec-induced distribution shift, not generalization to unseen speakers or unseen synthesis methods. This delimitation is discussed further in Section 5.3.
P250: 3.2.2 The “Robustness Pipeline” (Data Augmentation)
P251: To address the lossy-environment constraint, a custom transcoding pipeline was implemented using FFmpeg, wrapped via the pydub Python library. This stack was selected because it allows precise, reproducible bitrate control across codecs, so that the compression simulation is consistent with real-world VoIP and streaming conditions.
P252: Six codec conditions are defined:
P253: · Opus: 16 kbps (low-bandwidth), 32 kbps (standard), 64 kbps (high-fidelity)
P254: · MP3: 64 kbps and 128 kbps
P255: · AAC: 128 kbps
P256: The pipeline is used in two distinct roles, and the distinction matters for interpreting the results.
P257: As training augmentation. During training of the codec-augmented configuration (A1), each utterance is transcoded on the fly through a randomly selected condition with probability 0.5, and left unmodified otherwise. The objective is to force the model away from fragile high-frequency cues that compression destroys and toward artifacts that persist through re-encoding. Transcoding during training carries a measurable computational cost, as each on-the-fly FFmpeg invocation adds latency not present in the unaugmented baseline; this study did not retain a directly comparable timing log for the unaugmented configuration, so a precise throughput ratio is not reported.
P258: As an evaluation condition. For the robustness evaluation, the entire test partition was transcoded through each of the six conditions in advance and decoded back to waveform, producing six degraded copies of the test set alongside the original clean audio. Every model configuration was then evaluated on all seven versions. Because the same utterance identifiers appear in every condition, predictions across models and conditions are paired, which permits the paired significance testing described in Section 3.7.
P261: 3.3 Stream 1: The “Ear” (Raw Waveform Encoding)
P262: The first stream captures micro-temporal structure and timbral inconsistency through deep representation learning, discovering discriminative patterns from raw input rather than relying on hand-designed features.
P263: · Architecture: RawNet2 (Jung et al., 2020), a 1D convolutional neural network operating on raw audio.
P264: · Input: Raw waveforms, normalized and segmented into fixed-length 4-second frames at 16 kHz.
P265: · Mechanism: RawNet2 employs sinc-convolution layers whose filters are parameterized as band-pass filters. The network learns which frequency sub-bands carry synthesis artifacts without relying on pre-computed spectrograms. Residual blocks and a gated recurrent unit follow the sinc front-end.
P266: · Output: A 256-dimensional embedding vector, Eraw.
P270: 3.4 Stream 2: The “Microscope” (Statistical Signal Forensics)
P271: The second stream is the deterministic component, computing hand-crafted features to expose mathematical anomalies that convolutional networks may not represent explicitly.
P272: 3.4.1 Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (LFCC)
P273: MFCCs are standard for speech recognition but are designed to mimic human hearing, which attenuates high-frequency detail. Because deepfake artifacts are often concentrated in those bands, MFCCs would discard the evidence before the model sees it. LFCCs use a linear frequency scale and preserve the high-frequency spectral detail required for forensic analysis. This stream uses a 120-dimensional LFCC representation. Sixty coefficients are extracted per frame; the per-utterance mean and standard deviation of each coefficient are then computed across frames and concatenated, yielding a fixed-length 120-dimensional vector regardless of utterance duration. A total of 128 linearly-spaced filterbanks were used, with Type-II DCT, at a 16 kHz sample rate. Framing used a 400-sample (25 ms) analysis window with a 200-sample (50%) hop and a Hann window, following the extraction library’s default STFT parameters.
P274: 3.4.2 High-Order Statistics: Bispectral Analysis
P275: Building on AlBadawy et al. (2019), who observed higher-order spectral correlations in synthesized speech that are stronger than those in human speech, Stream 2 measures phase coupling as a candidate forensic cue. Phase coupling is the correlation between the phases of different frequency components. Quadratic phase coupling (QPC) specifically refers to the phase correlation between a sum component (f₁ + f₂) and its constituents (f₁, f₂).
P276: Phase information is discarded by the power spectrum used in conventional spectrograms, but the bispectrum retains it, exposing non-linearities in the signal generation process. Direct computation is prohibitive: the bispectrum of a full 4-second sample (N = 64,000) is O(N²), requiring over four billion elements and causing immediate memory exhaustion. An averaged-block estimator is therefore used. The signal is divided into M overlapping short-time windows (256 samples, 50% overlap), and the bispectrum is estimated by averaging the bi-periodograms of these segments, as given below.
P277: Each frame was weighted with a Hamming window prior to the FFT. The reported bispectrum magnitude, |B(f₁, f₂)|, is used directly, without log-scaling or bicoherence normalization.
P278: B(f₁, f₂) = (1/M) Σₘ Xₘ(f₁) · Xₘ(f₂) · Xₘ*(f₁ + f₂)
P280: where Xₘ(f) is the discrete Fourier transform of the m-th windowed segment, X* denotes the complex conjugate, and M is the number of segments.
P281: A non-zero bispectrum magnitude at specific bifrequencies indicates quadratic phase coupling, which AlBadawy et al. (2019) found to be stronger in synthesized than in human speech. Whether this indicator survives lossy compression is an empirical question addressed in Chapter 4.
P282: 3.4.3 Stream 2 Architecture
P283: The bispectral estimate is a matrix of one-sided bifrequency bins. With a 256-sample analysis window, the one-sided spectrum contributes 128 bins per axis, so the estimate is stored as a 128 × 128 matrix that flattens to 16,384 features. In the current implementation, which was used to train and evaluate every reported model, only the lower-triangular half of the valid region is computed (f₂ ≤ f₁ and f₁ + f₂ < 128), and the symmetric half is left at zero. This 16,384-dimensional vector creates a dimensionality mismatch relative to the compact LFCC vector, which risks the network overfitting the bispectral features or neglecting the LFCC input entirely.
P284: Principal Component Analysis (Jolliffe, 2002) is applied to the flattened bispectral vectors, retaining the top K = 128 principal components. The K = 128 PCA transform used by B0 and by every fusion configuration was not fitted on training speech. It was fitted on the bispectra of 500 synthetic Gaussian-noise waveforms (64,000 samples each, random seed 42), and at K = 128 it explains approximately 40.7% of the variance of those noise bispectra. It therefore functions as a fixed, data-independent linear projection from 16,384 to 128 dimensions, applied identically during training and evaluation, and should not be read as capturing the principal components of speech bispectra. Because the same projection is applied to every model that uses it and to every acoustic condition, the comparisons reported in Chapter 4 remain internally valid. The value K = 128 was chosen because it is comparable to the dimensionality of the LFCC vector. Balancing the dimensional sizes of disparate feature sets prevents any single modality from dominating the loss function during training (Baltrušaitis et al., 2018).
P285: The balanced feature set, consisting of concatenated LFCC and PCA-reduced bispectral features, is processed by a three-layer fully connected network (Figure 3.1) with a decreasing bottleneck structure: a 248-dimensional input (120 LFCC + 128 PCA-reduced bispectral), a first hidden layer of 256 nodes, a second hidden layer of 128 nodes, and a 64-node output layer. Each dense layer is followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation. The output is a 64-dimensional statistical embedding, Estat.
P288: Figure 3.1 Architectural topology of the three-layer MLP used in Stream 2.
P290: The PCA bottleneck also serves a computational purpose. Bispectrum extraction is O(N²) and is the dominant cost in this stream. Higher-order statistics matrices of speech signals are assumed to be sparse, with discriminative QPC artifacts concentrated in specific bifrequency interactions rather than distributed uniformly. Because the projection used here was fitted on synthetic noise rather than on speech, it is not guaranteed to retain those forensically relevant directions; its role in this study is dimensionality reduction rather than the selection of speech-specific structure. Feeding an uncompressed flattened matrix into the MLP would cause the first dense layer’s parameter count to grow by orders of magnitude. Compressing to K = 128 avoids this. This is a design rationale rather than a validated performance claim; no inference-latency measurements were collected in this study, and deployment feasibility is identified as future work in Section 5.4. 
P291: Reproducibility note. The bispectrum code changed during the project. The K = 128 projection (data/pca_model/pca.pkl) was produced by the --synthetic mode of scripts/fit_pca.py with an earlier implementation that computed the full valid region. All reported models were trained and evaluated with the current implementation described above, which computes only the lower-triangular half. The projection is fixed and is applied identically to every model and condition that uses it; the effect of this historical fit/feature mismatch, and of computing only half of the valid region, was not ablated.
P292: 3.5 Neuro-Symbolic Fusion Layer
P293: The fusion layer combines the two embeddings using a cross-modal attention mechanism in which the statistical embedding conditions the neural embedding.
P294: Mechanism. Inspired by attention architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017), the mechanism acts as a selective feature gate. The statistical embedding Estat serves as the query vector, guiding the model to weight the components of the raw-waveform embedding Eraw according to where statistical anomalies are strongest. The attention-weighted neural embedding is then concatenated with the statistical embedding to form a 320-dimensional fused representation (256 from Stream 1, 64 from Stream 2):
P295: Efused = Concat( Attention(Eraw; query = Estat), Estat )
P296: Both embeddings are first projected into a shared 256-dimensional space via separate linear layers. The statistical projection serves as the query in a 4-head scaled dot-product attention operation (embed_dim=256), attending over a two-token key/value sequence formed by stacking the raw and statistical projections. The attended output is normalized via layer normalization, concatenated with the statistical projection, and passed through a linear layer (512→256) with ReLU activation and dropout (p=0.2) to produce a 256-dimensional attention output, which is precisely the Attention(Eraw; query = Estat) term in the equation above, not yet the final fused representation. That 256-dimensional output is concatenated with Estat one step later to form the 320-dimensional Efused. The trainable parameters of the fusion layer are referred to hereafter as the fusion head.
P297: This ordering matters: attention is applied first, so that the statistical stream modulates the neural representation, and concatenation follows so that the statistical evidence is also preserved directly in the fused vector.
P298: A second configuration replaces the attention mechanism with plain concatenation of the two embeddings, holding all else constant by design. Comparing the two is intended to isolate the contribution of the attention mechanism itself.
P299: Classification. The fused vector is passed through a final linear layer with a sigmoid activation. Unlike softmax, which normalizes across mutually exclusive categories, the binary nature of this task dictates a single-node logistic transformation. The sigmoid maps the fused representation into a continuous score bounded between 0 and 1. Higher scores indicate that the model considers the utterance more likely to be spoofed, and lower scores indicate bona fide audio. Calibration of these scores was not evaluated, so they should not be interpreted as probabilities of spoofing. Labels are encoded accordingly, with 0 denoting bona fide and 1 denoting spoofed. The model is optimized with binary cross-entropy loss.
P302: 3.6 Training Configuration
P303: All models were trained on the 87,585-utterance training partition using NVIDIA T4 GPUs; the hardware is recorded in the surviving run records only for B1. The training script retains the checkpoint with the lowest validation EER observed during a run, and each reported model is the checkpoint retained under that policy. Except for B1, whose partial training logs survive, no per-epoch validation history was recovered, so this policy rather than an audited training record is the basis for each selection. Seed 42 was used for drawing the subsample and assigning the partitions (Section 3.2.1), for generating the synthetic waveforms and setting the random state of the PCA fit (Section 3.4.3), and for A1’s codec augmentation; no global training seed governing weight initialization, minibatch order or dropout was recorded for any run. Five primary configurations are evaluated in this study, summarized in Table 3.1, together with two supplementary runs described below.
P304: The training script optimizes with Adam and reduces the learning rate with a plateau-triggered scheduler (factor 0.5, patience 5 epochs on validation EER); the revision used for the fusion runs also applies a ten-epoch linear learning-rate warm-up. No scheduler state was saved with any checkpoint, so the schedule each run actually followed is not recorded. The checkpoint evaluated is the one saved with stored epoch index 12 (validation EER 5.66%), the lowest validation EER among the saved checkpoints of that run. B1 is the statistical stream with a K = 64 PCA projection that, like the K = 128 projection, was fitted on bispectra of 500 synthetic noise waveforms (Section 3.4.3); it was trained for ten epochs without augmentation, and the evaluated checkpoint was saved at the tenth (validation EER 6.04%). Neither run isolates a single factor. B1 differs from B0 in PCA size, in the bispectrum code version with which its PCA was fitted, in learning rate, and in the point in training at which its checkpoint was retained (B0’s full training log was not recovered), so it does not isolate PCA dimensionality. C1 was trained separately from C0, its streams differ from C0’s, and its exact training invocation is not documented, so the C0–C1 comparison does not isolate the fusion training strategy.
P309: Fusion training procedure and its provenance. The fusion configurations were designed to be constructed by transplanting trained stream encoders into the fusion architecture, freezing them, and training only the fusion head. The saved optimizer states of C1 and C2 include only fusion-head parameters, and both streams’ saved parameters and buffers, including BatchNorm running statistics, are identical between the two models. Together, these support both streams having been held frozen during the fusion-head training represented by these checkpoints; earlier training or optimizer-reset history cannot be verified. The data used to train the fusion heads is not recorded.
P310: The exact training invocation for C1 and C2 was not preserved in the project repository. Comparison against the A1 checkpoint shows that C1’s Stream 1 parameters are closely related to A1’s (cosine similarity 0.996 over the concatenated parameter vector, against a baseline of 0.075 for a randomly initialized encoder of the same architecture) but are not identical to it, indicating a distinct snapshot from the same Stream 1 training lineage. P313: 3.7 Evaluation Metrics
P314: To ensure comparability with standard anti-spoofing benchmarks, the following metrics are used:
P315: 1. Equal Error Rate (EER): The operating point at which the false acceptance rate equals the false rejection rate. EER measures threshold-independent ranking quality computed directly on the test scores; it does not represent accuracy or the expected error on arbitrary real-world audio. Lower is better. EER is the primary metric reported throughout Chapter 4.
P316: 2. Minimum Detection Cost Function (minDCF): The normalized minimum of the detection cost function, computed with the ASVspoof 5 Track 1 parameters (Delgado et al., 2024): spoof prior π_spf = 0.05, C_miss = 1, and C_fa = 10. minDCF is reported alongside EER for the seven-condition evaluation.
P318: The tandem detection cost function (t-DCF) was specified in the original research design. t-DCF evaluates a countermeasure jointly with an automatic speaker verification (ASV) subsystem and requires ASV scores as input. The system proposed here is a standalone countermeasure with no ASV component, which corresponds to Track 1 of the ASVspoof 5 challenge, for which minDCF rather than t-DCF is the designated primary metric. minDCF is therefore substituted, and the substitution is discussed in Section 5.3.
P320: 3. Ablation Study: To isolate the contribution of individual design choices, the following configurations are compared (Figures 3.2 to 3.4 show the three configuration families):
P321: · A1 against A0—the contribution of codec augmentation to Stream 1, holding architecture and data constant.
P322: · C1 against A1—the contribution of fusion relative to the strongest single stream. This comparison addresses Specific Problem 5 and is the central comparison of the study.
P323: · C1 against B0—the contribution of fusion relative to the statistical stream.
P324: · C2 against C1—the contribution of the cross-modal attention mechanism relative to plain concatenation.
P325: 4. Statistical Significance Testing: Because every configuration is evaluated on the same fixed test partition, predictions are paired at the utterance level, and McNemar’s test (McNemar, 1947; Dietterich, 1998) applies. The test evaluates the null hypothesis that two configurations have the same error rate, using only the utterances on which they disagree. The continuity-corrected form of the statistic (Yates, 1934; Edwards, 1948) is used throughout:
P326: χ² = (|n₀₁ − n₁₀| − 1)² / (n₀₁ + n₁₀)
P328: where n₀₁ is the number of utterances the first configuration classifies correctly and the second does not, and n₁₀ the reverse. A significance threshold of p < .05 is used throughout. A single implementation computes every p-value reported in this study.
P330: Alongside the p-value, both the χ² statistic and the raw discordant counts are reported for each comparison. The discordant counts matter because the number of disagreements varies substantially across acoustic conditions, and with it the statistical power of the test; two comparisons may share a p-value while differing greatly in effect size, or share an effect size while differing in significance purely through the number of errors available to compare. The χ² statistic is reported because it distinguishes comparisons that all round to the same p-value.
P331: Reporting convention. Where the χ² statistic is large, the survival function underflows to zero in double precision, so no meaningful exact p-value can be quoted. All p-values below .001 are therefore reported as p < .001, with the χ² statistic given alongside.
P332: Binarization. Model outputs are continuous scores; discrete predictions are obtained by thresholding at the operating point where the false acceptance and false rejection rates are equal. These thresholds are derived for each model–condition pair from that pair’s test scores, so they are analysis operating points for paired comparison and do not represent validated thresholds for real-world deployment. McNemar predictions were recomputed from saved test scores at each model–condition pair’s test-derived EER threshold. Because the scores recorded in the prediction files are rounded to six decimal places while the threshold was originally computed on full-precision scores, recomputing predictions from the recorded scores can differ on utterances lying immediately adjacent to the threshold. To ensure every reported comparison is internally consistent, all significance testing recomputes predictions from the recorded scores under a single implementation rather than reading previously stored labels.

P346: 4.1 Overview
P347: This chapter reports the evaluation of the two-stream detection system and discusses what the results support. The material is organized in the order the work was carried out: correction and validation of the data pipeline, baseline models trained on that corrected pipeline, the optimized fusion configurations, and the seven-condition robustness evaluation with paired significance testing.
P348: All figures in Sections 4.2 and 4.3 are validation-partition results, used for model selection. Every figure from Section 4.4 onward is computed on the held-out test partition (18,769 utterances), which was not used for training or checkpoint selection at any stage. Test-partition results are the basis for all claims in this study.
P349: Five primary configurations are evaluated, and two supplementary runs (B1 and C0) are reported in Sections 4.3 and 4.5. A0 is the raw-waveform stream trained without augmentation. P351: 4.2 Data Pipeline Validation
P352: An initial training run produced near-random discrimination, with all three configurations yielding EERs between approximately 36% and 41%. Diagnosis attributed this to the data pipeline rather than the model architecture. Three faults were identified: a data input/output bottleneck that throttled throughput to roughly 0.1 batches per second; an inverted training/validation split in which the validation partition was larger than the training partition; and a severe under-representation of the bona fide class in the training data.
P353: The pipeline was rebuilt to address each fault. A stratified subsample of 125,123 utterances was drawn from the ASVspoof 5 corpus using a fixed random seed (42), retaining all available bona fide utterances and drawing spoofed utterances evenly across attack types A01–A16 to preserve attack diversity. This subsample was partitioned 70/15/15 into training (87,585), validation (18,769), and test (18,769) sets, stratified on class label so that the bona fide proportion of approximately 40% is held constant across all three partitions.
P354: The partition assignment is reproducible and was verified before every evaluation session. A SHA-256 digest over the sorted utterance identifiers paired with their split assignments yields the value ed4808bb0456de26. This digest was recomputed and checked at the start of each evaluation run; a mismatch would indicate that the manifest no longer corresponded to the data the models were trained on, and would invalidate the results of that session. No mismatch occurred.
P355: Following these corrections, model performance improved by an order of magnitude, confirming that the original failure was a pipeline defect rather than a limitation of the architecture. This episode is reported because it directly shaped the final methodology and illustrates how sensitive deep audio models are to data-pipeline correctness.

The claim is an inference from the divergence between the two metrics and was not measured directly; per-class error rates at the minDCF threshold would be required to confirm it.

P428: The most likely explanation for the MP3 and AAC results involves the statistical stream. Among the five primary configurations, B0 has the highest error under every condition, and under MP3 its error nearly doubles, from 7.50% on clean audio to 13.43% at 64 kbps. The saved checkpoints support both streams having been held frozen during fusion-head training (Section 3.6), but the data used to train the fusion head is not recorded, so the following explanation is conditional. If the head was trained without codec augmentation, it never observed a degraded statistical embedding during training. Under MP3 and AAC compression the attention mechanism would then be asked to weight a statistical embedding whose reliability has changed in a way it has no basis to recognize, and the fused prediction would be drawn toward a stream that has become less informative than the one it was intended to complement. This account is consistent with the measurements but was not tested directly; a controlled test would require retraining the fusion head on codec-augmented audio, which is identified as future work in Section 5.4.

The consistency of direction across all seven conditions is suggestive, and a formal test of that consistency across conditions would be informative, but the per-condition evidence does not support a claim that the attention mechanism is necessary to the architecture. The advantage is established on clean audio and MP3 64k and should be stated as such.
P438: 4.5.4 Performance of the statistical stream
P439: Among the five primary configurations, B0 has the highest error under every condition and the largest absolute increase in error under compression, reaching 18.35% under Opus 16k. Both optimized fusion models outperform it by a wide margin under all seven conditions, with p < .001 throughout. The χ² statistics for C1 against B0 range from 1127.46 under AAC 128k to 2664.30 under Opus 16k, an order of magnitude above any other comparison in this study. Under Opus 16k the two configurations disagree on 3,369 utterances, of which C1 classifies 3,183 correctly and B0 186.
P440: Read against Chapter 2, these measurements qualify rather than overturn the premise drawn from AlBadawy et al. (2019), and the qualification depends on whether degradation is measured in proportional or absolute terms. Proportionally, the statistical stream is not the fragile component. Its EER rises by a factor of 1.01 to 2.45 across the six codec conditions, within a range comparable to the codec-augmented raw-waveform stream (0.96 to 2.96) and below that of the unaugmented raw-waveform stream A0 (1.01 to 6.14), which, like B0, was trained on clean audio only; B0’s factor is the smaller of the two in five of the six conditions, the exception being Opus 64k, where A0’s error is nearly unchanged (1.54% to 1.56%). Under Opus 64k and AAC 128k its error changes little (factors of 1.10 and 1.01), consistent with AlBadawy et al.’s report that bicoherence statistics remained discriminative after recompression at 64 kbps, and with Frank and Schönherr’s (2021) observation that an LFCC-based detector was more robust than RawNet2 under a simulated telephone channel. MP3 at comparable or higher bitrates degrades it markedly (factors of 1.71 to 1.79), a codec-family dependence the earlier work did not examine. In absolute terms, however, the statistical stream has the highest error of any configuration at every condition, and because it starts from a higher base, its absolute increase in EER is also the largest among the five primary configurations in five of the six codec conditions (10.85 percentage points under Opus 16k). Its limitation in this study is therefore discriminative power rather than compression durability. Three implementation choices could contribute, none isolated here: the stream uses the raw bispectral magnitude rather than the normalized bicoherence AlBadawy et al. used, the K = 128 PCA projection was fitted on synthetic Gaussian noise rather than on speech, so its retained variance on speech bispectra was not measured (Section 3.4.3), and the stream was trained on clean audio only.
P441: The supplementary configuration B1 replaces B0’s K = 128 projection with a K = 64 projection, also fitted on synthetic noise, and reaches 6.04% validation EER and 7.58% test EER on clean audio (Table 4.5). McNemar’s test at each model’s test-derived EER threshold finds no significant difference between B1 and B0 on clean audio (p = .74; EER difference +0.08 percentage points), Opus 16k (p = .93), Opus 64k (p = .054), or AAC 128k (p = .099), and a significant advantage for B1 under Opus 32k, MP3 64k, and MP3 128k (p < .001). This comparison is confounded: B1 differs from B0 in PCA size, in the bispectrum code version with which its PCA was fitted, in learning rate, and in the point in training at which its checkpoint was retained (Section 3.6). The improvement under those three conditions therefore cannot be attributed to PCA dimensionality, and B0 and B1 remain the two weakest configurations under every condition.
P443: Figure 4.4 minDCF by model across the seven acoustic conditions, logarithmic vertical axis. 
P444: Figure 4.4 is the minDCF counterpart to Figure 4.2 and reports the values tabulated in Table 4.4. B0’s separation from the remaining configurations is wider here than under EER, which is the divergence discussed in Section 4.4.1. As in Figure 4.2, C1 and C2 nearly coincide at several conditions; the overlap reflects their near-identical performance rather than a rendering defect.

To characterize the uncertainty in the underlying EER and minDCF estimates themselves, 95% confidence intervals were computed using bootstrap resampling. For the EER intervals in Table 4.9, 1,000 resamples were drawn with replacement at the utterance level, using a single shared set of resampling indices across all five primary configurations so that between-model comparisons remain paired in the same way as the McNemar tests above. The minDCF intervals in Table 4.10 were computed from a separate 2,000-resample nonparametric bootstrap over the same test partition, as recorded in the note beneath that table. P457: 4.6 Summary of Findings
P458: The evaluation covered five primary configurations under seven acoustic conditions on a held-out test partition of 18,769 utterances, with paired significance testing throughout.
P459: Codec augmentation of the raw-waveform stream is the largest effect measured. It improves performance under every condition tested, including clean audio, with reductions in error of 35% to 74% and p < .001 in all seven cases. This result rests on A1’s documented training invocation and an unaugmented control designed to match it (Section 3.6).
P460: Fusion of the two streams improves on the best single stream under clean audio and all three Opus conditions, with a proportional advantage that is stable at roughly 1.34 to 1.43 across those four conditions. Under MP3 compression the advantage disappears and the point estimate mildly favors the single stream. Under AAC the direction favors fusion but does not reach significance. The improvement, where present, is significant but modest. Both fusion configurations outperform the statistical stream by a wide margin under every condition.
P461: Cross-modal attention (C1) attains lower error than plain concatenation (C2) under all seven conditions, though the difference reaches significance under only two of them (clean audio and MP3 64k), so attention cannot be claimed as necessary on the present evidence.
P462: Taken together, the results indicate that robustness in this system comes principally from codec-augmented training of the raw-waveform stream, with architectural fusion contributing a smaller improvement that holds on clean and Opus-compressed audio and does not extend to MP3 or AAC. Chapter 5 discusses the implications, limitations, and directions this suggests for further work.
P463: Chapter 5
P464: CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
P465: 5.1 Summary of the Study
P466: This study designed and evaluated a two-stream neuro-symbolic architecture for deepfake audio detection under lossy compression. The architecture combines a raw-waveform stream based on RawNet2 with a statistical stream computing linear-frequency cepstral coefficients and PCA-compressed bispectral features, fused either by concatenation or by a cross-modal attention mechanism in which the statistical embedding conditions the neural embedding.
P467: Five primary configurations were trained on a stratified 125,123-utterance subsample of ASVspoof 5 and evaluated on an in-domain held-out test partition of 18,769 utterances under seven acoustic conditions: clean audio and six codec conditions spanning Opus, MP3, and AAC at varying bitrates. All comparisons were made on identical utterance sets, permitting paired significance testing with McNemar’s test.
P470: 5.2 Conclusions
P471: The study set out to answer six specific questions. Each is addressed below against the evidence in Chapter 4.
P472: On the degradation of detectors under lossy compression (Problem 1). Degradation is substantial but highly uneven across codec families and configurations. The unaugmented raw-waveform baseline (A0) degrades from 1.54% EER on clean audio to 9.48% under Opus at 16 kbps, a factor of six, while degrading only marginally under AAC at 128 kbps (1.62%). Bitrate alone does not predict degradation: A0 performs worse under MP3 at 128 kbps (5.87%) than under Opus at 64 kbps (1.56%), despite the higher nominal bitrate. Codec family and bitrate interact, and evaluating robustness against a single codec would have produced a misleading picture.
P473: On the vulnerability of specific cue types (Problems 2 and 3). The statistical stream has the highest error of any configuration under every condition, although its proportional degradation under compression is within the range of the codec-augmented raw-waveform stream and below that of the unaugmented one in five of six codec conditions. B0 degrades from 7.50% on clean audio to 18.35% under Opus 16k and 13.43% under MP3 64k. The raw-waveform stream trained with codec augmentation remains below 3% under every condition. The gap is the largest measured in this study: the χ² statistics for the fusion model against the statistical stream range from 1127.46 under AAC to 2664.30 under Opus at 16 kbps, an order of magnitude above any other comparison reported here. The premise drawn from AlBadawy et al. (2019), that phase-coupling evidence survives recompression, holds within the range that study tested and is bounded beyond it: under Opus at 64 kbps and AAC at 128 kbps the statistical stream’s error changes little from clean audio (factors of 1.10 and 1.01), whereas low-bitrate Opus and MP3 degrade it substantially. What the stream does not provide, in this implementation, is absolute discrimination comparable to the learned waveform representation it was designed to complement.
P474: This finding should be stated carefully. It does not establish that phase-coupling artifacts are destroyed by compression. It establishes that this particular implementation of bispectral analysis, computed with the windowing and noise-fitted PCA configuration described in Section 3.4 and trained on clean audio, does not retain its discriminative power after low-bitrate Opus or MP3 re-encoding. No Mel-scale (MFCC) counterpart was trained, so the relative stability of linear-scale and Mel-scale cepstral features raised in Problem 2 was not tested directly. Whether a bispectral stream trained under codec augmentation would fare differently was not tested and is identified below as the most valuable single follow-up experiment.
P475: On raw-waveform degradation across codec families (Problem 4). Both raw-waveform configurations degrade most severely under Opus at 16 kbps, the lowest bitrate tested, which is consistent with the expectation that aggressive bandwidth reduction removes the high-frequency detail on which vocoder fingerprints partly depend. However, the ordering across families is not monotonic in bitrate, as noted above. Codec-augmented training substantially flattens this profile: A1 varies only between 0.96% and 2.95% across all seven conditions, against A0’s range of 1.54% to 9.48%.
P476: On whether fusion improves robustness (Problem 5). Fusion improves on the strongest single stream under clean audio and all three Opus conditions, and does not under MP3 or AAC. Across the four conditions where the improvement holds, the ratio of paired disagreements favoring the fusion model is stable between 1.34 and 1.43, and statistical significance within that group tracks the total number of disagreements rather than the size of the advantage. The improvement is therefore best characterized as a constant proportional effect measured at differing statistical power, not as an effect that strengthens under compression.
P477: This revises the expectation stated in Chapter 1. The absolute margin in EER does widen as conditions worsen, from 0.19 percentage points on clean audio to 0.57 under Opus 16k, but this follows from applying a constant proportional advantage to a larger number of errors rather than from fusion becoming more valuable under compression.
P478: Under MP3 the effect reverses in direction, with the single stream attaining marginally lower EER at both bitrates, though neither difference reaches significance. The conclusion is that fusion provides no measurable benefit under MP3 compression. Under AAC the direction favors fusion but the result does not reach significance, and with a single AAC condition tested this study cannot distinguish a genuinely reduced effect from sampling variation.
P479: Cross-modal attention (C1) attains lower EER than plain concatenation (C2) under all seven conditions, and the paired disagreements favor attention in every case, but the difference reaches significance under only two of them: clean audio and MP3 at 64 kbps. The two configurations disagree on between 47 and 93 utterances out of 18,769, so the per-condition tests have limited power; under Opus at 32 kbps the disagreements split 35 to 34 and the test statistic is effectively zero. The consistency of direction across seven independent conditions is suggestive, but the per-condition evidence does not support a claim that attention is necessary to the architecture.
P480: On codec augmentation compared with fusion (Problem 6). Codec-augmented training is the dominant source of robustness in this system. The comparison between A1 and A0 is designed to isolate it: the two share the architecture and data and differ by design only in whether transcoded variants were presented during training, although minor procedural differences between their training runs cannot be excluded (Section 3.6). A1 outperforms A0 under every condition, with reductions in error between 35% and 74% and p < .001 in all seven cases. The χ² statistics for this comparison scale with the severity of the compression, from 30.91 on clean audio to 873.37 under Opus at 16 kbps, indicating that augmentation contributes most where the codec is most destructive. Architectural fusion, by comparison, contributes an improvement of 0.16 to 0.57 percentage points where it holds at all, and does not hold under three of the seven conditions.
P481: The practical implication is that a practitioner building a compression-robust detector under resource constraints would obtain considerably more benefit from augmenting the training distribution than from adding a second forensic stream. This was not the expected ordering at the outset of the study.
P482: A secondary observation deserves note. Codec augmentation improved clean-condition performance as well, from 1.54% to 1.00% (p < .001). Augmentation is often expected to trade clean accuracy for robustness, and no such trade-off appeared. A plausible reading is that transcoding acts as a general regularizer, discouraging the model from relying on fine spectral detail that is unstable even in uncompressed recordings. This was not tested directly.
P483: Overall. The proposed architecture achieves 0.81% EER on clean audio and remains below 2.4% under every codec condition tested on the in-domain test partition, which satisfies the general objective of a detector that remains reliable in lossy environments within the known-attack, known-speaker scope of this evaluation. The mechanism responsible, however, is not the one the study anticipated. Robustness derives principally from codec-augmented training of the raw-waveform stream, with the two-stream fusion contributing a smaller improvement that holds on clean and Opus-compressed audio and does not extend to MP3 or AAC.
P484: These conclusions are drawn from EER, which describes performance at the balanced operating point. They are corroborated at a security-oriented operating point by minDCF, computed with the ASVspoof 5 Track 1 cost parameters, which reproduces the same model ordering in six of the seven conditions and yields a comparable proportional advantage for fusion over the single stream wherever that advantage holds (Section 4.4.1). The single ordering disagreement, under MP3 at 64 kbps, is marginal in both directions and consistent with the conclusion that the two configurations are effectively tied under MP3. The findings reported here are therefore not artifacts of the operating point at which performance was measured.
P485: 5.3 Limitations
P486: Known-attack evaluation protocol. The partitions form an utterance-level random split of the ASVspoof 5 training and development data, so attack types A01 through A16 and speakers appear in both the training and test partitions of the subsample used here. The evaluation therefore measures in-domain robustness to codec-induced distribution shift under known attacks and speakers, and does not measure generalization to unseen speakers or unseen synthesis methods. Results are not directly comparable to the official ASVspoof 5 challenge, whose evaluation partition withholds attack types from training. Detectors typically perform substantially worse under unknown-attack conditions, and the EER values reported here should be read as an upper bound on what this architecture would achieve under the official protocol.
P487: Single training run per configuration. Each configuration was trained once, with no repeated runs, and no global training seed was recorded. No variance estimates across training runs are therefore available. This is material to the smaller comparisons in this study. The fusion advantage over the single stream is 0.16 to 0.19 percentage points under the milder conditions, and the difference between the attention and concatenation fusion heads is smaller still, resting on disagreements numbering in the tens of utterances. Differences of this magnitude could plausibly fall within seed-to-seed variation, and the present design cannot rule that out. The McNemar tests establish that the paired predictions differ more than chance would explain for these particular trained models; they do not establish that a retrained pair would differ in the same direction.
P488: Incomplete training provenance for the fusion configurations. The exact training invocation used to produce the C1 and C2 checkpoints was not preserved in the project repository. Post-hoc analysis of the saved checkpoints establishes that the saved optimizer states of C1 and C2 include only fusion-head parameters and that both streams’ saved parameters and buffers are identical between the two models, which supports both streams having been held frozen during the fusion-head training represented by these checkpoints, and that C1’s Stream 1 parameters are closely related to the A1 checkpoint (cosine similarity 0.996 over the concatenated parameter vector, against 0.075 for a randomly initialized encoder of the same architecture) without being identical to it. The evidence therefore supports the conclusion that the fusion models were built on a frozen Stream 1 encoder from the same training lineage as A1, but does not identify the specific source checkpoint. A0 and A1 were likewise produced by different revisions of the training script, and the batch size and learning rate that most runs actually used are documented only partly or inferred from the saved optimizer states (Table 3.2). Consequently the account offered in Section 5.2 of how the fusion models inherit codec robustness is supported by architectural evidence rather than by a training record.
P489: This limitation is disclosed rather than omitted because it bears directly on reproducibility. All metrics, tables, and figures reported in this study are reproducible from the committed prediction outputs and the hash-verified partition manifest by running the two build scripts in the project repository, and the clean-condition results were independently regenerated from the checkpoints in a separate session to confirm that the evaluation pipeline is deterministic. Regeneration from the checkpoints themselves requires retrieving them through Git LFS, which a fresh clone cannot currently do because the repository’s LFS bandwidth quota is exhausted; the checkpoints are committed and their contents are unaffected, but reproduction from them depends on that quota being restored. The path that produced two of those checkpoints, moreover, is not fully recoverable. The practical lesson is that training invocations must be committed as artifacts alongside the checkpoints they produce; in this project, load-bearing configuration repeatedly existed only in interactive notebook cells, and one training log survived only because it had been deliberately written to the repository.
P490: Substitution of minDCF for t-DCF. The original research design specified the tandem detection cost function. t-DCF scores a countermeasure in tandem with an automatic speaker verification subsystem and requires ASV scores as input. The system proposed here is a standalone countermeasure with no ASV component, corresponding to Track 1 of the ASVspoof 5 challenge, for which minDCF rather than t-DCF is the designated metric. minDCF was therefore computed instead. This is a correction of the research design rather than a shortfall against it, but it does mean results are not directly comparable to published t-DCF figures for tandem systems.
P491: No latency measurement. Chapter 3 argues that the PCA bottleneck mitigates the O(N²) cost of bispectral extraction. No inference-latency measurements were collected, so this remains a design rationale rather than a validated property. Claims about real-time deployability are outside what this study can support.
P492: Limited codec coverage. Six conditions across three codec families were tested. AAC is represented by a single bitrate, which prevents the anomalous AAC result from being interpreted confidently. Real distribution channels apply codec chains, resampling, loudness normalization, and repeated re-encoding, none of which were simulated.
P493: Fixed segment length. All audio was processed as 4-second segments. Performance on shorter utterances, and the behavior of the bispectral estimator when fewer windows are available for averaging, were not examined.
P494: Statistical-stream feature pipeline. The K = 128 PCA projection used by B0 and by every fusion configuration was fitted on bispectra of 500 synthetic Gaussian-noise waveforms rather than on speech, and it explains approximately 40.7% of the variance of those noise bispectra. It acts as a fixed, data-independent reduction applied identically to every model and condition that uses it, so the reported comparisons remain internally valid, but the statistical stream was never given a projection informed by speech. The projection was also fitted before a change to the bispectrum code: it was fitted on full-region bispectra, whereas every reported model was trained and evaluated on features from the current implementation, which computes only the lower-triangular half of the valid bifrequency region. Neither the noise fitting nor this historical fit/feature mismatch was ablated, and both bear on the interpretation of the statistical stream’s weak discriminative power in Section 4.5.4.
P495: Analysis thresholds and score calibration. The thresholds used to binarize scores for McNemar’s test are EER operating points derived from each model’s test scores under each condition (Section 3.7); McNemar predictions were recomputed from saved test scores at each model–condition pair’s test-derived EER threshold. These thresholds support paired comparison between models but are not validated decision thresholds, and calibration of the model scores was not evaluated. No claim is therefore made about the error rate of a deployed system operating at a fixed threshold, or about performance on audio outside the in-domain test partition.
P496: 5.4 Recommendations for Future Work
P497: Train the fusion head under codec augmentation. This is the most direct test of the explanation offered for the MP3 and AAC results. The data used to train the fusion head in this study is not recorded; if it was trained on clean audio, it never observed a degraded statistical embedding. If the account in Section 4.5.2 is correct, retraining the fusion head on codec-augmented audio should allow the attention mechanism to learn when the statistical stream has become unreliable, and should recover the fusion advantage under MP3 and AAC. If it does not, the explanation is wrong and the limitation lies deeper in the statistical features themselves. Either outcome is informative, and the experiment requires no new data.
P498: Train the statistical stream under codec augmentation. The finding that the statistical stream discriminates weakly, and degrades under low-bitrate Opus and MP3, was measured on a stream trained exclusively on clean audio. Given that codec augmentation transformed the robustness profile of the raw-waveform stream, the same treatment applied to Stream 2 is a necessary control before concluding that phase-coupling evidence is inherently compression-fragile.
P499: Sweep the PCA dimensionality of the statistical stream. K = 128 was selected on dimensional-balance grounds, and the projection used was fitted on synthetic noise rather than on speech (Section 3.4.3). The one additional value tested, K = 64 in the supplementary configuration B1, was also fitted on synthetic noise and differs from B0 in more than PCA size (Section 4.5.4), so it does not isolate the effect of dimensionality. Whether the statistical stream’s weakness under compression is partly a function of that choice, rather than of the bispectral features themselves, is untested. A sweep across K would establish this and is inexpensive relative to the other experiments proposed here.
P500: Fit the PCA projection on training speech. The projection used in this study was fitted on bispectra of synthetic Gaussian noise. As proposed future work, the projection would be refitted on bispectra of training-partition speech computed with the current bispectrum implementation, and the statistical stream retrained with it, to establish whether a speech-informed projection improves its discriminative power. Neither the refitting nor the retraining has been performed. A K = 64 projection fitted on 2,000 real audio samples exists in the project repository but was not used by any reported model, so no real-data PCA comparison is reported in this study.
P502: Repeat training across multiple seeds. Reporting mean and variance across several runs per configuration would establish whether the fusion advantage, and the attention-versus-concatenation difference, survive seed-to-seed variation. Given the size of these effects, this is the one change that would do most to strengthen the study’s central claims.
P503: Evaluate under the unknown-attack protocol. Using the official ASVspoof 5 evaluation partition, with attack types withheld from training, would establish how far these findings extend from codec robustness to generalization robustness, and would make the results comparable with published work.
P504: Broaden codec coverage. Additional AAC and MP3 bitrates would resolve whether AAC constitutes a genuinely distinct regime or a sampling artifact. Chained and repeated encodings, resampling, and platform-specific processing would move the evaluation closer to real distribution conditions.
P505: Measure inference latency. Establishing the real-time factor of each configuration would allow the deployability argument in Chapter 3 to be evaluated rather than assumed, and would quantify the cost of the second stream against the benefit measured here.
P506: Investigate the codec-family dependence directly. The divergence between Opus and MP3 or AAC is the most surprising result of this study and remains unexplained. Examining how each codec’s transform and quantization strategy affects the bispectral estimate specifically, rather than only its effect on end-to-end detection error, would clarify whether the pattern reflects a property of the features, of the fusion mechanism, or of the codecs themselves.
